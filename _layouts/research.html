Research

Most of what I do comes back to two questions. When does an operation genuinely introduce something new into the world, rather than rearrange what was already there? And once something has been introduced, what fixes its identity? The four threads below run from metaphysics into musical ontology and back out again, and the last of them is where the account meets fields that build structural descriptions of their own.

I

Objects and Structures in Music

Musical ontology has largely taken works as the privileged target of theorizing, filtering a diverse set of phenomena through a historically contingent category and leaving improvisation- and recitation-based traditions undertheorized. The papers here start further down, with musical elements and the operations that relate them.

“Music without Works: A Case for Compositional Ontology”

Forthcoming The Philosophical Quarterly (2026)  ·   ·   ·  Link

Contemporary musical ontology has become fixated on musical works, treating them as the primary—often exclusive—subject of metaphysical inquiry. This paper argues that such work-centrism is methodologically limiting. Musical work is a heterogeneous category that resists unified ontological treatment, while the very concept of a work proves historically contingent and practice-relative. Moreover, work-centrism leaves un(der)theorized vast ranges of musical phenomena that merit ontological consideration. These limitations are diagnosed from testing the work-centric methodology against four desiderata for an ontological theory of music, each motivated by reflecting on musical phenomena and practices. I then propose a compositional approach to musical ontology, focused on musical elements (pitches, rhythms, motifs) and their interrelations under compositional operations, as a promising alternative. This approach not only meets the desiderata but also systematically tracks musical content—what makes something musical—thereby offering a stable foundation for philosophical theorizing about music as such.

A paper presenting and defending a trifold (platonist) musical ontology

In preparation

The platonist position in musical ontology faces a familiar puzzle: music seems created, historically situated, and intention-dependent, yet platonism treats musical objects as abstract and mind-independent. Attempts to preserve both often either deny that works are created or revise the orthodox conception of abstracta. This paper argues that this tension is illusory and proposes a trifold ontological framework on which the illusion dissolves: (i) a musical space of structural objects; (ii) pathways through that space constructed by composers; and (iii) presentations (performances, scores) that manifest these pathways. This distinction relocates the intuition of creation to pathways and presentations, while reserving the structural resources they utilize as uncreated abstracta. By analyzing the formal features of musical objects—specifically their multiplicity, invariance, and inheritance—I demonstrate that platonism offers the most compelling account of musical structure. This ontology respects both the abstract identity of music and the contingent, creative practices of its history.

II

Generating Objects and Structures

Working in the other direction, from music back into general metaphysics, I take operations rather than objects as the primary thing to theorize about: an ontology expands by applying generative operations to what is already in its domain, and the structures they induce are what give us a view of the ontological space. The question driving this thread is what makes an operation generative at all, and what the answer commits us to about the identity of what it generates.

A paper developing a general theory of generativism

In preparation

Generativism holds that objects enter our ontology by being generated from others via certain operations. Much work addresses specific cases—sets, mereological fusions, neo-Fregean abstractions—yet a foundational question persists: what makes an operation generative? Existing approaches localize generativity to particular applications, appealing to grounding, real definition, or essence, but each fails to generalize across the full range of paradigmatic cases. I argue that generativity is a global, structural feature of an operation's systematic applicative behavior. Working within a higher-order framework with plurals and a Finean logic of essence, I propose that an operation is generative when it is saturable—its applicative profile admits coherent refinement in every context so that every admissible behavior is uniquely realized—and every realized behavior traces a generative path affording the output an essential identity-explanation. The framework provides a unified account of iterative, compositional, and abstraction-based generation, showing that what appear to be heterogeneous modes of ontological formation are instances of the same structural phenomenon.

A paper arguing for a top-down structuralist approach to essences

In preparation

Non-eliminative structuralism struggles to reconcile the idea that mathematical objects are "positions in structures" with rigorous accounts of their identity. Dominant bottom-up approaches—generating positions via abstraction and reading off structural identities—work for sets but encounter friction with rigid and non-rigid structures pulling in different directions. I propose a top-down alternative: treat morphisms as metaphysically prior to objects, generate the structure itself first, then derive the positions it warrants. The paper introduces structural saturability as a criterion for generativity and develops Yoneda Essence, an essentialist interpretation of the Yoneda Lemma: an object's identity is exhaustively constituted by the network of transformations it supports. Taking the natural numbers as paradigm, I show how a canonical number structure is generated as the unique initial object from homomorphisms between discrete dynamical systems. The approach treats rigid and non-rigid cases uniformly and offers a rapprochement: generativity tracks essences in a structural way.

III

Form, Meaning, and Abstraction

Form here is not a specifically musical notion. If a form is a structure of directed transitions rather than an inventory of parts, the account should reach wherever something is encountered as unfolding — music, film, performance. Running alongside is the converse of the question the generativist work asks: not what an operation confers on what it generates, but what an abstraction retains, and what the standard answer quietly gives away.

“Schopenhauerian Musical Formalism: Meaningfulness without Meaning”

Published Journal of Comparative Literature and Aesthetics 46 (4), 70–79 (2023)  ·   ·   ·  Link

In this paper I develop a version of musical formalism inspired by Schopenhauer. First, I present a Schopenhauerian account of music with a background of his metaphysical framework. Then, I define meaningfulness as an analog to a Kantian notion of purposiveness and argue that, in light of Schopenhauer, music is meaningful as a direct manifestation of the universal will. Given the ineffable nature of what music points to, its form lacks any representation of meaning. Music is therefore the mere form of meaningfulness, and it is precisely this mere form that gives rise to the infinite possibilities to ascribe it with a variety of musical meaning.

A paper on musical formalism, movement, and meaning

Under review

Hanslick’s declaration that music’s content is tönend bewegte Formen (“sonically moving forms”) harbors an underappreciated tension: Formen suggests rigidity, bewegte insists on dynamism, tönend demands experiential accessibility. Standard formalisms privilege one component at the expense of others. I propose a reconstruction in which movement is primary. A musical form is a category whose objects are musical elements and whose morphisms encode directed transitions—leading, departing, resolving—between them. Since a category is individuated by its morphisms, a form is determined by its transition-pattern rather than any particular inventory of named elements, making the framework culturally neutral. Three kinds of musical movement are distinguished, with two further modes—enrichment and expansion—introduced via functors. The resulting formalism explains how meaning and affect emerge from compositional structure: music is expressive because its transition-structure maps, via structure-preserving functors, onto the dynamic contours of experiential processes, without reducing music to propositional content.

A paper on the nature of aesthetic form

In preparation

Aesthetic formalists distinguish temporal from spatial arts by whether formal relations unfold in time or coexist in space, a distinction that is phenomenologically salient but not, I argue, ontologically fundamental. This paper uses film to make the case that aesthetic form is neither a temporal nor a spatial configuration but a pattern of transformation: a structure of possible movements objectively present in a work and manifested only in a viewer’s navigation through it. La Jetée (1962), composed almost entirely of stills, is the case the argument is built on. If the account holds, it generalizes past film, and the temporal/spatial division turns out to mark a difference in how forms are encountered rather than a difference in what forms are.

A paper on symmetries, abstraction, and identification of objects

In preparation

A central question in my dissertation is what an operation confers on what it generates; this paper takes up the converse, what an abstraction retains. Abstractionists have converged on a condition governing what passes from specifications to abstracta: a relation among specifications may be inherited by a predicate of abstracta only if it holds or fails uniformly across all specifications of one and the same abstractum. I call this policy Proscriptive Inheritance, and argue that it leaves an abstractum able to inherit a group of symmetries while losing the means to individuate what those symmetries act on. The policy is well motivated where the subject matter is purely logico-mathematical; enforcing it indiscriminately across domains is a substantive and currently undefended decision. Music is where I make the cost concrete.

Musical Interpretation as Construction

Collaborative

With a pianist and scholar of performance pedagogy. What is a performer doing when she interprets a piece? On the picture inherited from the nineteenth-century ideal of Werktreue, she recovers a determinate work fixed in the score and realizes it as faithfully as she can. We argue that a score underdetermines any particular route through the musical resources it makes available, that constructing that route is the interpretive act itself, and that the construction is expressive in its own right. Post-tonal repertoire is the clearest test case, since without a thick layer of inherited performance convention there is nothing for recovery to recover.

IV

Structure Where Formalization Runs Out

The metaphysical insights afforded by the Yoneda Lemma reach beyond philosophy of mathematics. If an object has identity essentially explained in terms of its arrow profile, then recognizing the natural number structure is not categorically different from recognizing, say, a movement posture like the Tai Chi “Cloud Hands,” a musical style, or the organization a living system maintains across changes of substrate. The three collaborations here pursue that in fields where structural description is already the working method. Each puts pressure on the same question from a different side: what a structural description captures, and what it leaves out.

Generative Ecologies: Tweaks, Leaps, and Blocks

Collaborative

With collaborators in network science, complexity science, and developmental psychology. When a new form is realized — a musical subgenre, a technology, a biological lineage — does it open up room for further forms, or does it close the space down and lock the field into a monoculture? The group models this as a birth-and-death process on a network whose nodes are individual forms, deriving whether two forms compete or reinforce one another from how far their neighborhoods overlap. My argument is that overlap alone cannot settle the sign. Competition and synergy correspond to different patterns of behavior, and telling them apart calls for a form-space with types, which in turn requires an account of which operations compose and which subsume one another. The cases run from the evolution of metal subgenres to dinosaur lineages to the morphology of sea shells.

Enactive Metaphor and Tacit Knowledge Transmission

Collaborative

With collaborators in philosophy and cognitive science. Skilled practitioners routinely know things they cannot say, and teachers transmit those things anyway, usually by reaching for an image. We argue that a teaching image projects a sparse relational constraint rather than a specification of movement, which is why a single image can reach joints it never names, and why learners of different proportions can satisfy the same instruction without their bodies ever matching. A distinction the literature tends to collapse is between an image that draws on a quality the learner can already recognize on its own (as with “dough is ready when it feels like an earlobe”) and one that establishes a resemblance only in being enacted (as with “move your hands like clouds”), leaving nothing behind afterwards except the possibility of enacting it again.

Closure and Causation in Living Organisms

Collaborative

With a complexity scientist. Relational biology, following Robert Rosen, characterizes living systems as closed with respect to efficient causation: every process the system needs in order to keep itself going is produced within the system. Accounts in this tradition tend to reach for further causal notions — constraint, measurement, control among them — and assert them alongside efficient causation. I argue that they should instead be derived from the closure condition itself. The derivation turns on that condition being impredicative: closure is the fixed point of a generative operation, which is exactly the structure my general theory of generativism is built to handle.

Dissertation

Objects, Structures, and Abstraction

This dissertation consists of five standalone papers, with a prelude, interludes, and a postlude marking the transitions between them. It runs in two directions at once — from metaphysics into the ontology and formalism of music, and from music back into the metaphysics of objects and structures generally — held together by a single thesis about how operations introduce and individuate the objects of an ontology. Each chapter below appears, up to isomorphism, among the papers above.
